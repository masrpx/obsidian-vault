## 1. #392 – Genetic testing: when it’s valuable, how to choose the right test, and what to do with the results
- source: https://peterattiamd.com/genetictesting/
- type: rss
- vault_path: 04 Resources/Biohacking
- relevance: As a clinic owner scaling to 20 branches, Max needs a rigorous framework for evaluating genetic testing services his clinic may offer or recommend to patients — separating high-signal tests (BRCA, APOE, pharmacogenetics) from low-evidence functional medicine upsells, protecting both patient outcomes and clinic credibility.
- approved: true

Genetic testing is far more nuanced than most people assume — DNA is probabilistic, not deterministic, and directly measuring phenotype often provides more actionable data than inferring risk from genotype alone. High-value use cases include BRCA/cancer syndromes, inherited cardiac conditions, APOE for Alzheimer's risk awareness, and pharmacogenetics for medication optimization. Functional medicine genetic tests (methylation, detox pathways, supplement protocols) lack clinical evidence and should be treated skeptically. The practical framework: only pursue genetic testing when there is a clear clinical question, an appropriate test matched to that question, and a result that can meaningfully change a decision or behavior.

## 2. A critical look at ultra-processed diets and male reproductive health
- source: https://peterattiamd.com/ultra-processed-diets-and-reproductive-health/
- type: rss
- vault_path: 04 Resources/Biohacking
- relevance: With twin daughters arriving mid-2026, Max is likely in or near the conception/pre-conception phase — sperm quality, hormonal health (FSH), and metabolic optimization are directly actionable biohacking targets; also aligns with his interest in diet-performance research and clinic (Ratchatakaya) patient education on metabolic health.
- approved: true

A randomized crossover feeding study (n=43 men, 20–35yo) found that 3 weeks on an ultra-processed food (UPF) diet caused ~1.3–1.4kg fat mass gain, worsened cardiometabolic markers (LDL:HDL, blood pressure), and in the excess-calorie arm, reduced FSH (~0.5 IU/L) and total sperm motility (~13%) — effects observed across both adequate and excess calorie conditions. The NOVA classification defines UPFs as industrial formulations using refined ingredients, additives, and flavor enhancers (packaged snacks, sweetened cereals, pastries, etc.). The central unresolved question is whether UPFs exert direct calorie-independent biological harm on reproductive hormones and spermatogenesis, or whether effects are primarily mediated through excess energy intake and metabolic dysfunction. Headlines (e.g., Washington Post) overstated causality; the study design, while stronger than observational data, has limitations in fully isolating processing from calorie composition. Practically, minimizing UPFs appears prudent for metabolic and reproductive health optimization, especially relevant during pre-conception windows.
