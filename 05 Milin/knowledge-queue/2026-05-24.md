## 1. #392 – Genetic testing: when it’s valuable, how to choose the right test, and what to do with the results
- source: https://peterattiamd.com/genetictesting/
- type: rss
- vault_path: 03 Resources/Biohacking
- relevance: Max runs 9 clinics scaling to 20 and has biohacking interests — understanding when genetic testing adds real clinical value vs. when it's noise is directly applicable to patient advisory, clinic service offerings, and Max's own longevity optimization stack. The framework for evaluating test actionability also maps well to his INTJ systems-thinking approach.
- approved: true

Genetic testing is not a blueprint for predicting all future diseases — its value depends entirely on the clinical question being asked. Some tests are genuinely life-altering (e.g., BRCA for cancer risk, APOE for Alzheimer's, inherited cardiac conditions), while others like functional medicine panels (methylation, detox genes) lack clinical evidence despite biological plausibility. A key principle: directly measuring the phenotype is often more informative than inferring risk from genotype alone. Peter's framework evaluates tests by effect size and actionability — only pursue genetic testing when it's matched to a specific question, uses the appropriate test type, and can meaningfully change a decision or behavior.

## 2. A critical look at ultra-processed diets and male reproductive health
- source: https://peterattiamd.com/ultra-processed-diets-and-reproductive-health/
- type: rss
- vault_path: 03 Resources/Biohacking
- relevance: Max is biohacking-focused and will be a father of twin girls mid-2026 — male reproductive health, hormonal optimization, and diet quality are directly actionable. Understanding UPF impact on FSH and sperm quality connects to longevity, metabolic health, and performance optimization within his biohacking framework.
- approved: true

A randomized crossover feeding study in 43 men found that 3 weeks on an ultra-processed diet (UPFs) caused ~1.3–1.4 kg fat mass gain, worsened cardiometabolic markers (LDL:HDL, blood pressure), a ~0.5 IU/L drop in FSH, and ~13% decline in total sperm motility — effects that appeared in both adequate- and excess-calorie conditions. These findings suggest UPFs may disrupt male reproductive hormones and spermatogenesis independently of total caloric intake, not merely through overeating. However, a critical lens is warranted: the study involved only 43 non-obese men over 3 weeks, and separating 'ultra-processed' as a causal mechanism from confounders like overall diet quality remains methodologically challenging. The broader context is alarming: meta-analyses report ~50% decline in sperm concentration and ~60% drop in total sperm count globally between 1973–2018, with diet as a key modifiable variable. The central unresolved question is whether 'ultra-processed' itself is a causal mechanism or a proxy label for diets that drive metabolic dysfunction.
