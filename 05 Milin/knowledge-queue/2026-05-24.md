Genetic testing is not a universal blueprint for future health — its value depends entirely on asking the right question, choosing the right test, and having actionable options based on results. High-value use cases include BRCA cancer risk, inherited cardiac conditions, APOE for Alzheimer's risk awareness, and pharmacogenetics for medication optimization. Directly measuring phenotype (biomarkers, imaging) is often more clinically useful than inferring risk from DNA alone. Functional medicine genetic testing (methylation, detox pathways, supplement protocols) largely lacks evidence and should be approached skeptically. Peter's framework: evaluate any genetic test by effect size + clinical actionability before ordering it.

## 2. A critical look at ultra-processed diets and male reproductive health
- source: https://peterattiamd.com/ultra-processed-diets-and-reproductive-health/
- type: rss
- vault_path: 03 Resources/Biohacking
- relevance: Max is a biohacker and clinic owner (Ratchatakaya, 9 branches) with twin daughters arriving mid-2026 — male reproductive health, hormonal optimization, and diet-metabolism interactions are directly actionable both personally and potentially for his clinic's wellness protocols.
- approved: false

A 50% drop in sperm concentration and 60% decline in total sperm count has been documented globally between 1973–2018, with ultra-processed food (UPF) diets emerging as a key suspect. A randomized crossover feeding study (n=43 men, ages 20–35) found that just 3 weeks on a UPF diet caused ~1.3–1.4 kg fat mass gain, worsened cardiometabolic markers (LDL:HDL, diastolic BP), a ~0.5 IU/L drop in FSH, and ~13% decline in total sperm motility — even when calories were controlled. However, the study has notable limitations: small sample size, short intervention window (3 weeks is insufficient to complete a full spermatogenesis cycle of ~74 days), and the two dietary patterns differed substantially in macronutrient composition (fiber, protein, fat ratios), making it hard to isolate 'processing' as the independent variable versus overall diet quality. The core open question remains: are UPFs causally harmful to reproductive biology, or are they simply a proxy for excess calorie intake, poor micronutrient density, and metabolic dysfunction?
