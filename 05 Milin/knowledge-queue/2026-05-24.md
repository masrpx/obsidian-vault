## 1. #392 – Genetic testing: when it’s valuable, how to choose the right test, and what to do with the results
- source: https://peterattiamd.com/genetictesting/
- type: rss
- vault_path: 03 Resources/Biohacking
- relevance: Max's biohacking focus likely includes genetic/biomarker testing as part of health optimization; this framework helps him avoid low-signal consumer genetic tests and prioritize high-actionability ones (e.g., APOE, BRCA, pharmacogenetics) — directly applicable when managing his own health stack and advising patients at Ratchatakaya Clinic.
- approved: false

Genetic testing is powerful but widely misunderstood — its value depends entirely on asking the right clinical question first. Some findings are deterministic and life-altering (e.g., BRCA mutations, inherited cardiac conditions, APOE for Alzheimer's risk), while most are probabilistic and require phenotype measurement to be meaningful. Peter Attia's framework: evaluate any test by its effect size and clinical actionability — if the result won't change a decision, the test adds no value. Functional medicine genetic testing (methylation, detox pathways, supplement protocols) largely lacks clinical evidence despite biological plausibility. Pharmacogenetics — using DNA to guide medication selection — is one of the more immediately actionable applications.

## 2. A critical look at ultra-processed diets and male reproductive health
- source: https://peterattiamd.com/ultra-processed-diets-and-reproductive-health/
- type: rss
- vault_path: 03 Resources/Biohacking
- relevance: Max is biohacking-focused and will soon be a father of twin girls (mid-2026) — understanding diet's impact on hormonal and reproductive health is directly relevant, and the nuanced critique of study methodology aligns with his INTJ analytical mindset for evaluating health claims before acting on them.
- approved: false

Sperm concentration has dropped ~50% and total sperm count ~60% between 1973–2018, with ultra-processed foods (UPFs) emerging as a key dietary suspect. A randomized crossover feeding study (n=43 men) found that a 3-week UPF diet caused ~1.3–1.4 kg fat mass gain, worsened cardiometabolic markers (LDL:HDL, blood pressure), reduced FSH by ~0.5 IU/L, and dropped total sperm motility by ~13% — even when calories were controlled. However, critical limitations apply: the 3-week window is far shorter than the ~74-day sperm development cycle, the sample was small and non-obese young men only, and the dietary arms differed in fiber, micronutrients, and additives simultaneously — making it impossible to isolate which UPF feature is the actual mechanism. The headline claim that UPFs directly harm male sex hormones independent of calories is not fully supported by the study design; metabolic dysfunction from calorie surplus and poor diet quality remains a plausible confounding pathway.
